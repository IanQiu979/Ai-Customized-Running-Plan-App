Economy · Cadence · Harmony · Optimization

ECHO IS NOT A TRAINING PLAN. ECHO IS A COACHING PHILOSOPHY.
The four pillars work together to create a coaching system that
no algorithm can replicate. Every response, plan, and piece of

================================================================
PILLAR 1: ECONOMY (Energy Efficiency)
================================================================

DEFINITION:
Economy is the ability to move efficiently — consuming less oxygen
at a given pace. It is the foundation of every runner's long-term
potential.

WHY IT MATTERS:
Two runners with identical VO2 max and lactate threshold can have
vastly different race results if one is more economical. Economy
is trainable and improves over years of consistent training.

PHILOSOPHY:
Economy asks: 'Are you working smarter, or just harder?'

Economy separates the intuitive runner from the coached runner:
- Intuitive: 'I ran hard, so it's good'
- Coached: 'I ran that pace with lower HR, so I improved'

HOW ECONOMY IS MEASURED:

IMPORTANT: Heart Rate Zones Vary by Individual
Heart rate responses differ significantly between runners based on:
  • Age (younger runners typically have higher max HR)
  • Fitness level (trained runners have lower resting HR)
  • Training experience (experienced runners are more efficient)
  • Genetics (natural variation in HR response)
  • Medication and health status

A 25-year-old at 6:30/km pace may have HR of 145 bpm.
A 55-year-old at the same pace may have HR of 160 bpm.
A highly trained 35-year-old may have HR of 130 bpm.
All three may be running in Zone 1 (easy) for THEIR individual baseline.

DO NOT use age-predicted zones (e.g., "220 - age").
Instead: Establish individual heart rate baselines through testing.

> ℹ️ **NOTE — INTERNAL CONTRADICTION, SCOPED BY RULING (Ian, 2026-07-10):** This instruction contradicts `training_zones.md`, which builds all of its zone tables on 220 − age. Ian — shown Tanaka et al. 2001 (208 − 0.7 × age is more accurate; ±10-12 bpm scatter around any age formula) — ruled to keep 220 − age as the estimate *where no individual data exists*. That is exactly V2.2's situation: a one-time 8-field intake with no logging, so age is the only HR input available. Both positions stand, scoped: 220 − age is the no-data default; this pillar's individual-baseline principle applies whenever longitudinal HR data exists (Ian's live coaching, any future product with run logging).

Economy Measurement Methods:
1. Heart rate at a given pace (declining HR week-to-week = better economy)
   → Track individual's own trend, not against age-predicted targets
2. Perceived effort (same pace feels easier over time = better economy)
   → "This pace felt hard last week, easy today" = economy gain
3. Oxygen consumption (lower O2 at same pace = better economy)
   → Measured via VO2 testing (not available in app, but principle applies)

LONG-TERM ECONOMY GAINS COME FROM:
1. Running more (volume builds economy over 5-10 years)
2. Running efficiently (correct form, cadence, rhythm)
3. Recovery (fatigue destroys economy; rest improves it)
4. Age-specific adaptation (younger runners gain faster; masters runners maintain)

AGE-SPECIFIC ECONOMY APPLICATION:
- Runner age 25: Improving economy 3-5% per year (aggressive gain window)
- Runner age 55: Maintaining economy (focus is maintenance, not decline)
Coaching them the same way misses individual optimization.

COACHING THROUGH ECHO ECONOMY:

Post-Run Feedback (DO THIS):
'Your easy run today was at the same pace as last week, but your
HR was 4 bpm lower. That is improved economy — your body is
adapting. Keep this up.'

NOT this:
'Good pace, nice run.'

Easy Run Economy Rule:
Easy runs (Zone 1) are 60-70% of all training. If economy is poor,
easy runs feel hard. Economy improvement means easy runs feel
genuinely easy — this is the psychological breakthrough every
runner needs.

Post-Run Economy Alert:
'You ran 5:45 pace but your heart rate was 158 bpm. For you,
Zone 1 should be around 130-145 bpm based on your baseline.
This means this "easy" run was at Zone 2 intensity. Easy runs
need to be EASIER. Running easy runs too hard is the #1 cause
of injury and overtraining.'

PILLAR 2: CADENCE (Rhythm & Frequency)
================================================================

DEFINITION:
Cadence is steps per minute (SPM). It is the rhythm of running.

CADENCE TARGETS — WITH INDIVIDUAL VARIATION:

CRITICAL: Cadence is NOT one-size-fits-all.
Optimal cadence depends on:
  • Leg length (longer legs naturally run at lower cadence efficiently)
  • Biomechanics (individual stride mechanics)
  • Running efficiency (some athletes are efficient at 168 SPM, others at 185)
  • Experience level (beginners often need to build up gradually)

General Guidelines (NOT absolute rules):
- Easy runs (Zone 1): 170-180 SPM (but range is 160-190)
- Race pace: 175-185 SPM (but range is 165-195)
- Hard efforts (Zone 3-4): 185-195 SPM (but range is 175-200)
- Strides / max efforts: 190+ SPM (but range is 180-210)

> ℹ️ **EVIDENCE NOTE (per Ian's 2026-07-10 cadence ruling):** This pillar's individual-baseline position is the evidence-supported one. The intervention with trial support is a **5-10% step-rate increase above the runner's own baseline** (Br J Sports Med 2022 step-rate meta-analysis), and the ubiquitous 180-SPM figure traces to Jack Daniels counting elite runners at the 1984 Olympics — an observation of elites, never a prescription. Be aware the library contradicts itself: `training_zones.md`'s "Cadence Targets by Zone" table and the injury patterns in `load_rules.md` Rule 6 / `injury_flags.md` still prescribe absolute SPM floors; those spots carry matching annotations. Where the two positions collide, this pillar's is the evidence-aligned one.

Your baseline cadence should be established through:
1. Testing your comfortable running cadence (natural stride)
2. Gradually increasing IF form improves AND no new injuries appear
3. Respecting your individual biomechanics

⚠️ CADENCE INJURY WARNING FOR BEGINNERS:
Too-high cadence (forced increase above natural rhythm) can cause:
  • Shin splints (overloading anterior tibialis)
  • Stress fractures (excessive impact frequency)
  • Calf strains (overuse of lower leg muscles)
  • Hip/IT band issues (changed muscle recruitment patterns)

Safe cadence progression:
  • Start with NATURAL cadence (no forced changes)
  • Increase by 2-3 SPM per week ONLY if it feels natural
  • Stop increasing if new pain/tightness appears
  • Allow 4-8 weeks of adaptation before major increase
  • Work with a coach on form changes alongside cadence changes

WHY CADENCE MATTERS:

LOW CADENCE CONSEQUENCES (<160 SPM, if too low for individual):
- Overstriding (landing in front of center of gravity)
- Braking force (body slows on each impact)
- Heel striking (high impact on knees and joints)
- Result: Injury risk, slower pace, wasted energy

HIGH CADENCE BENEFITS (for your individual optimal range):
- Shorter, quicker strides
- Landing under center of gravity (no braking)
- Midfoot strike (efficient, reduced impact)
- Result: Injury reduction, improved economy, faster pace at same effort

PHILOSOPHY:
Cadence is largely CONTROLLABLE within your individual range —
but NOT infinitely so. Forcing cadence far above your natural rhythm
is counterproductive and injury-prone. The goal is to find YOUR
optimal cadence and maintain it consistently.

CADENCE IMPROVEMENT THROUGH DRILLS (Long-Term Development):

Cadence changes require 8-16 weeks of consistent work, not 3 minutes.
These drills build the neuromuscular patterns needed for sustainable
cadence increases.

DRILL 1: METRONOME RUNS (Build Cadence Awareness)
Duration: 20-30 min easy runs, 1x per week
Method:
  1. Establish baseline cadence through counting (count footsteps for 15 sec × 4)
  2. Use metronome app set to target cadence (start at +2-3 SPM above baseline)
  3. Run 10 min at metronome cadence, then 5 min without
  4. Repeat 2-3 cycles
  5. Finish with 5 min easy (natural cadence)

Progression (weekly):
  Week 1-2: Metronome at baseline + 2 SPM
  Week 3-4: Metronome at baseline + 4 SPM
  Week 5-6: Metronome at baseline + 6 SPM
  Week 7+: Increase 2 SPM per 2 weeks if cadence feels natural

Expected outcome: After 8 weeks, new cadence should feel automatic.

DRILL 2: STRIDES WITH CADENCE FOCUS (Neuromuscular Activation)
Duration: 20-30 sec each, done 2-3x per week (post-easy run)
Method:
  1. Do 6-8 strides (20-30 second fast runs with recovery walk)
  2. During strides: Focus on QUICK LEG TURNOVER (not speed)
  3. Cue: "Quick, light feet — turn legs over fast"
  4. Cadence during strides should be +10-15 SPM above easy run target

Why this works: Strides teach nervous system to activate at higher cadence
without fatigue (they're short). Easy runs become cadence reinforcement.

DRILL 3: SHORT HILL REPEATS FOR CADENCE (Forced High Cadence)
Duration: 1-2x per week, 6-8 repeats
Method:
  1. Find a gentle hill (3-5% grade)
  2. Run up hill 45-60 seconds at hard effort
  3. Natural consequence: Cadence increases on hills (shorter strides needed)
  4. Focus: Maintain cadence while climbing (don't slow legs down)
  5. Recover downhill or flat walking between repeats

Why this works: Hills naturally force cadence up. Nervous system learns
higher turnover is sustainable. Flat runs after hills feel "fast cadence."

DRILL 4: RUNNING DRILLS (Coordination & Cadence)
Duration: 10-15 min, 2x per week, before hard workouts
Drills (30-40m each, walk back, repeat 3-4x):
  1. High knees: Drive knees up to hip height, fast cadence (190+ SPM feel)
  2. Butt kicks: Kick heels toward glutes, quick rhythm (185+ SPM)
  3. A-skips: Exaggerated skip with high knee drive (190+ SPM feel)
  4. B-skips: Skip + back leg extension, powerful cadence (185+ SPM)
  5. Bounding: Exaggerated stride, explosive (180+ SPM feel)
  6. Single-leg hops: Activate glutes and calf, high cadence effort

Why this works: Drills teach the neuromuscular pattern of fast cadence
in a controlled way. Cadence learned in drills transfers to running.

DRILL 5: FARTLEK WITH CADENCE EMPHASIS (Variable Cadence Practice)
Duration: 30-40 min, 1x per week
Method:
  1. Easy 10 min warm-up
  2. Alternate: 2 min easy (baseline cadence) + 2 min hard (target cadence)
  3. Repeat 6-8 times
  4. Focus: Feel the difference between cadence targets
  5. Easy 10 min cool-down

Why this works: Runner learns to consciously control cadence at different
paces. Variability builds robust cadence control.

CADENCE PROGRESSION TIMELINE:
Week 1-2: Establish baseline, start metronome runs
Week 3-4: Add strides, minor cadence increase (2-3 SPM)
Week 5-8: Add hill repeats, see 4-6 SPM increase
Week 9-12: Consolidate gains, running drills, strides become automatic
Week 13-16: Maintain new cadence on all easy runs, should feel natural

Expected outcome: 4-8 SPM increase over 16 weeks if done consistently.
Larger increases require more time or risk injury.

REAL-TIME COACHING CUES (During Running):

Cue to increase cadence (DO THIS):
'Your cadence is 168 SPM, and you are landing heavily. Increase by
3-5 SPM over the next 3 minutes. Think: shorter, lighter steps. Feel
like you are floating.'

NOT this:
'Run faster.'

Why the difference matters:
The runner is not trying to run faster — they are turning legs over
faster. Shorter strides at higher frequency = same pace, less effort,
better form, lower injury risk.

Post-Run Cadence Feedback:
'Your cadence was strong for the first 30 minutes (178 SPM), but
dropped to 172 SPM in the final 10 minutes due to fatigue. This is
normal. Next run, focus on maintaining cadence when your legs start
feeling heavy — that is when it matters most. Use the cue: "Quick
feet" when cadence drops.'

CADENCE DROP UNDER FATIGUE = WARNING SIGNAL:
Cadence drop means running form is degrading. Form degradation =
injury risk + efficiency loss. Teaching cadence maintenance under
fatigue is teaching injury prevention + mental toughness.

RETURN FROM INJURY CADENCE PROTOCOL:
Runner returning from injury often has cadence 160-165 SPM due to
pain avoidance overstriding.
Coaching: 'Let's gently increase to 170, then 175 over 3-4 weeks
using cadence drills. This reduces impact forces and protects your
recovery. No forcing it.'

PILLAR 3: HARMONY (Movement Quality & Balance)
================================================================

DEFINITION:
Harmony is the integration of posture, arm swing, forward lean, and
body awareness. It is the flow of running — when movement looks and
feels right.

WHY IT MATTERS:
Harmony is not about beauty. It is about sustainability.
Poor harmony → Injury → Layoff → Deconditioning
Good harmony → Sustainable training → Continuous progress → Performance

PHILOSOPHY:
Harmony asks: 'Does your body move as one system, or as disconnected
parts? Are you running efficiently, or fighting gravity?'

HARMONY COMPONENT 1 — POSTURE (Head to Toe):

- Head: Eyes forward, chin level (not tucked down, not tilted up)
  Purpose: Neutral spine, allows efficient arm swing

- Shoulders: Relaxed, not raised or hunched
  Common error: Tension (especially when tired) raises shoulders
  Cue: "Drop shoulders, shake out hands every 5 min"

- Arms: 90-degree elbow bend, compact frame, NO crossing midline
  Purpose: Efficient force transfer to legs
  Common error: Arms cross body = energy wasted laterally
  Cue: "Arms move front-to-back in a box, not side-to-side"

- Torso: Straight line from head to hips, 5-10 degree FORWARD LEAN
  from ankles (NOT from waist/bending at waist)
  
  ⚠️ FORWARD LEAN IS CRITICAL FOR RUNNING EFFICIENCY:
  
  Without forward lean (running upright):
    • Fighting gravity on each step
    • Overworking lower back and leg muscles
    • Less efficient propulsion
    • Higher injury risk (especially lower back)
    • Slower pace at same effort
  
  With proper forward lean (slight, from ankles):
    • Gravity assists propulsion
    • Efficient force transfer from glutes/quads
    • Reduced muscular effort
    • Better economy and speed
    • Lower injury risk
  
  How to find it:
    1. Stand upright, feet hip-width apart
    2. Imagine standing against a wall behind you
    3. Tilt whole body forward 5-10 degrees (from ankles, not waist)
    4. Feel like you are falling forward and running to catch yourself
    5. This forward lean should feel like "controlled falling"
  
  Common error: Bending at waist (forward lean from waist, not ankles)
  Result: Inefficient, back pain, poor form
  
  Cue during running: "Fall forward, let gravity help"

- Hips: Tall, forward, stable pelvis (no hip drop, no swaying side-to-side)
  Purpose: Direct force application, no energy wasted
  Common error: Hip drop on single leg = weak glutes
  Solution: Prehab (glute bridges, side-lying leg lifts, lateral lunges)

- Feet: Land under center of gravity, midfoot strike (not heel)
  Purpose: No braking force, efficient propulsion
  Result: Higher cadence naturally leads to midfoot landing

If one element breaks (e.g., hunched shoulders), the whole system suffers.
Harmony is a chain; each link matters.

HARMONY COMPONENT 2 — FORWARD LEAN (Standalone Emphasis):

Forward lean is so critical it deserves its own section.

WHAT IS FORWARD LEAN?
A slight tilt of the entire body from the ankles (not the waist).
The body leans forward 5-10 degrees as a single unit.
This creates a falling sensation that is caught by forward leg motion.

WHY FORWARD LEAN MATTERS:
1. Gravity works WITH you (propulsion assist)
2. Muscles do less work at same pace (better economy)
3. Forces more efficient stride (midfoot landing)
4. Reduces lower back strain
5. Enables proper cadence (can't run high cadence upright)

WHAT FORWARD LEAN IS NOT:
❌ Bending at the waist (C-curve of spine)
❌ Hunching over
❌ Tilting head forward while upright

FORWARD LEAN DRILL (Learn the Feeling):
1. Stand against a wall, feet 30cm from wall
2. Tilt body forward from ankles until hands touch wall (lean ~15 degrees)
3. Feel this angle in your body
4. Return to upright
5. Repeat 10 times — memorize the sensation
6. Apply this 5-10 degree lean during running

Cue: "Feel like you're falling forward, running to catch yourself"

HARMONY COMPONENT 3 — ARM SWING (Upper Body Drives Lower Body):

Arm swing is not cosmetic—it directly powers leg speed.

How arm swing works:
- Arms directly drive leg speed and stride power
- If arms are stiff, legs will be stiff too
- Strong arms accelerate legs
- Weak arms = weak legs (same effort feels slower)

Proper arm swing mechanics:
- 90-degree elbow angle (compact, efficient)
- Drive elbows BACK (not swinging across body)
- Forward swing: hands reach sternum height (not higher)
- Back swing: hands reach hip level
- Hands relaxed, not clenched
- Cue: "Imagine holding a crisp (potato chip) without crushing it"

THE ARM SWING BOX (Visual Memory Aid):

Imagine a vertical rectangular box in front of your torso:
  ┌──────────────────────┐
  │    (Shoulders)       │  ← Top of box (shoulder height)
  │  ┌────────────────┐  │
  │  │  ARM SWING BOX │  │
  │  │   (Front view) │  │
  │  └────────────────┘  │
  │    (Hips)           │  ← Bottom of box (hip level)
  └──────────────────────┘

Arms should NEVER leave the box:
- Don't reach across the midline (outside edges of box)
- Don't reach above shoulders (top of box)
- Don't reach below hips (below box)
- Stay within front-to-back motion ONLY

If arms exit the box:
  → Energy wasted laterally or vertically
  → Rotational forces on spine (injury risk)
  → Less efficient propulsion
  → Slower pace at same effort

Cue: "Arms stay in the box — forward-back, not side-to-side"

Arm swing under fatigue:
When tired, arm swing often becomes sloppy. Re-engaging arm swing
re-activates legs. This is a powerful race/workout cue.

Racing cue: "Drive the arms hard. Strong arms wake up tired legs."

HARMONY COMPONENT 4 — HIP STABILITY (Core & Glute Strength):

Hip stability is built OFF the track, not during runs.

Why hip stability matters:
- Glutes stabilize the pelvis and hips during running
- Weak glutes → hip drop on each step → cascade of injuries:
  • Knee pain (Q-angle increases)
  • Ankle issues (proprioception decreases)
  • Lower back pain (unstable core)
- Hip stability enables midfoot landing and high cadence

Prehab exercises for hip stability (2x per week, non-running days):
1. Glute Bridges: 3 sets × 12 reps (build basic glute activation)
2. Single-Leg RDL: 3 sets × 8 each leg (balance + glute strength)
3. Lateral Lunges: 3 sets × 10 each side (hip abductor strength)
4. Clamshells: 3 sets × 15 each side (external hip rotators)
5. Side-Lying Hip Lifts: 3 sets × 12 each side (hip abductors)
6. Monster Walks: 3 sets × 15 each direction (glute activation)

Progression (8-12 weeks):
- Weeks 1-4: Bodyweight exercises only, focus on form
- Weeks 5-8: Add resistance (band, light weight)
- Weeks 9-12: Increase reps or weight while maintaining form

Expected outcome: Hip stability → form improves → injury prevention.

COACHING THROUGH ECHO HARMONY:

Form Assessment Feedback (DO THIS):
'Your posture is strong head-to-toe, forward lean is good, but your
right shoulder is raised compared to left. This is likely tension or
fatigue. You are also exiting the arm swing box on the right side.
Next run: every 5 minutes, consciously drop shoulders, shake hands,
and cue "arms in the box." This will improve your harmony.'

NOT this:
'Your shoulders look tight.'

Post-Run Harmony Feedback:
'Your harmony was excellent for the first 30 minutes: relaxed shoulders,
forward lean solid, arms in the box, nice cadence. In the final
10 minutes you fatigued: shoulders hunched, forward lean reduced,
arms starting to cross body. This is normal under fatigue.
Next long run, when you feel this happening, cue "posture reset":
drop shoulders, lean forward, drive arms back. This keeps harmony
intact even when tired.'

HARMONY UNDER FATIGUE:
Harmony is hardest to maintain when tired. This is where the runner
builds real resilience and learns mental toughness.

Coach cue: 'Even when tired, maintain your harmony. Posture. Forward
lean. Arm swing in the box. That is what separates trained runners
from untrained runners. That is the race.'

HARMONY VIDEO FORM ANALYSIS:
When runners upload form videos, analyze:
1. Forward lean (5-10 degrees? Bending at waist? Upright?)
2. Arm swing (staying in box? Crossing midline? Appropriate height?)
3. Shoulder position (relaxed or tense? Raised?)
4. Cadence (quick, light feet? Or heavy, slow?)
5. Hip stability (hip drop? Stable pelvis?)
6. Head position (eyes forward? Tucked?)

Feedback should address what to IMPROVE, not just what is wrong.
'Your forward lean is reduced in the final stretch. Add 2-3 degrees
more forward tilt. This will help when you fatigue.'

HARMONY RECOVERY EXAMPLE:
Female runner, 45 years old, returning after 2-year break.
Harmony is gone — movement looks stiff, uncoordinated, no forward lean.

Coaching plan:
- Weeks 1-2: Daily mobility work (rope stretching, yoga, foam rolling)
- Weeks 2-3: Form-focused easy runs (5-10 min deliberate form work focusing
  on forward lean and arm swing box, then 20 min easy running reinforcing it)
- Weeks 4-6: Strides 3x per week (20-30 sec fast runs practicing harmony
  under higher cadence; easier to maintain form at short duration)
- Weeks 7-8: Prehab 2x per week (glute bridges, RDLs, lateral work)

Result by week 8: Harmony restored, movement fluid, proper forward lean,
arm swing in box, zero injuries. Ready for longer, harder running.

PILLAR 4: OPTIMIZATION (Pacing & Energy Distribution)
================================================================

DEFINITION:
Optimization is smart pacing — distributing energy throughout a run
or race to maximize performance output.

WHY IT MATTERS:
Most runners do not optimize. They run hard when they should run easy,
and easy when they should run hard. They blow up in races.
Optimization separates disciplined runners from emotional runners.

PHILOSOPHY:
Optimization asks: 'Are you running this effort, or is this effort
running you?'

TRAINING ZONES (Updated with Clarification):

Heart rate zones differ by individual (as noted in Economy section).
Use INDIVIDUAL baselines, not age-predicted values.

Zone definitions by perceived effort and physiology:

Zone 1 (Easy/Recovery): 60-70% max HR
  Perceived effort: Conversational, very easy
  Purpose: Base building, recovery, volume
  Physiology: Aerobic system developed

Zone 2 (Steady/Aerobic): 70-80% max HR
  Perceived effort: Comfortable, but breathing elevated
  Purpose: Threshold building, long-term aerobic gains
  Physiology: Right at lactate threshold boundary

Zone 3 (Tempo/Hard): 80-90% max HR
  Perceived effort: Hard, can speak in short phrases
  Purpose: VO2 max, lactate threshold
  Physiology: At or above lactate threshold

Zone 4 (Very Hard): 90-95% max HR
  Perceived effort: Very hard, can say 1-2 words
  Purpose: VO2 max work, short intervals
  Physiology: Significantly above lactate threshold

Zone 5 (Maximum Effort): 95-100% max HR
  Perceived effort: All-out, cannot speak
  Purpose: Sprint work, near-max efforts
  Physiology: Maximum aerobic power

⚠️ Zone 5 is ONLY used for short sprints and maximum efforts.
Not for sustained workouts. 30-60 second maximum efforts.

Zone 2's physiology also differs ("right at lactate threshold boundary" here vs "marathon pace to slightly faster" there). A generator emitting HR ranges needs one authoritative set. Which governs?

THREE PACING STRATEGIES:

1. EVEN SPLIT (same pace throughout):
   Best for: Experienced runners with well-tested goal pace
   Psychology: Predictable, controllable, builds confidence
   Risk: If starting pace is even slightly too fast, you blow up

2. NEGATIVE SPLIT (slower first half, faster second half) — RECOMMENDED:
   Best for: Most runners, safest universal strategy
   Psychology: Confidence builds as race/run progresses
   Benefit: Push hard when fresher, strong finish feels powerful
   Risk: Requires discipline not to start too fast with adrenaline

3. POSITIVE SPLIT (faster first half, slower second half) — AVOID:
   Used by: Runners who do not follow a plan
   Why it happens: Race adrenaline causes too-fast start
   Result: Burnout, suffering, missed goal time
   Psychology: Momentum loss as pace slows

OPTIMIZATION CUES BY SESSION TYPE:

Easy Runs (Zone 1):
'Comfortable effort. You can hold a full conversation. No racing.
No effort. This is patience training.'
Message: Discipline is the skill. Resist ego. Trust the process.

Steady Runs (Zone 2):
'Sustainable effort. Breathing elevated, but not struggling.
You should be able to hold this pace for 45-90 minutes.'
Message: Building aerobic capacity takes time.

Tempo/Threshold Work (Zone 3):
'Hard effort, but controlled. You can say a few words if asked.
Hold this effort for 20-40 minutes total (including repeats).'
Message: This is where fitness improves fastest.

Hard Intervals (Zone 3-4):
'Push hard for the interval duration. Recovery is for recovering.
During intervals: Aggressive effort.'
Message: Attack when opportunity calls.

Maximum Efforts / Sprints (Zone 5):
'ALL OUT. This is 30-60 seconds, maximum intensity.
Everything you have.'
Message: Brief, intense efforts train power and neuromuscular system.

Long Runs (Zone 1-2, negative split):
'Start easy, finish strong. Stay Zone 1 for first 60-75% of run.
Final 10-20 minutes: pick up to Zone 2. Negative split the finish.'
Message: Patience early is strategy. Aggression late is power.

Races:
'Even effort first half (or slightly conservative). Push hard final
quarter. Control early. Attack late.'
Message: The race is won in the second half.

POST-RUN OPTIMIZATION FEEDBACK:

Easy Run Optimization Issue:
'You ran 5:45 pace but heart rate was 158 bpm. For your baseline,
Zone 1 should be 130-145 bpm (6:15-6:30 pace). This easy run was
at Zone 2 intensity. Running easy runs too hard is the number one
cause of runner injury and overtraining. Next easy run: slow down
by 30-45 seconds per km. It should feel almost too easy.'

Hard Effort Optimization Feedback:
'Your first two intervals were 5:35 pace. Your last two dropped to
5:52 pace. This 17-second gap is your current fitness ceiling.
That ceiling is exactly what we are training to raise. Next interval
session, aim to hold all intervals within 5 seconds of each other.
That is controlled aggression — discipline under fatigue.'

MARATHON TRAINING PROGRESSION (McMillan-Based, Accurate Timeline):

⚠️ CRITICAL: Marathon training requires 16-30 weeks MINIMUM.
Most runners need 24-30 weeks for proper preparation.
9-week timelines are NOT safe and lead to injury/poor performance.

Proper progression (30-week McMillan model):

PHASE 1 — BASE BUILDING (8 weeks):
Goal: Build aerobic foundation, establish consistency
Work: 70% easy running, 20% steady, 10% long runs
Weekly mileage: Build from current to peak (gradual increase)
Example: 30 km/week → 35 → 40 → 45 → 50 km/week
Long run: Build from current to 15-18 km

PHASE 2 — BUILD PHASE (8 weeks):
Goal: Increase aerobic capacity, introduce threshold work
Work: 60% easy, 30% steady/tempo, 10% long runs
Weekly mileage: 50-60 km/week
Hard sessions: 1-2 per week (steady runs, tempo work)
Long run: Build 18 → 20 → 22 → 24 km

PHASE 3 — STRENGTH PHASE (4 weeks):
Goal: Build speed, lactate threshold, VO2 max
Work: 60% easy, 20% tempo, 20% hard intervals
Weekly mileage: 55-65 km/week
Hard sessions: 2-3 per week (intervals, tempo repeats, hills)
Long run: 24-26 km (maintain, add goal-pace work at end)

PHASE 4 — RACE-SPECIFIC TRAINING (6 weeks):
⚠️ CRITICAL: Only after base, build, and strength phases are complete
Goal: Train at GOAL MARATHON PACE with goal-race scenario simulation
